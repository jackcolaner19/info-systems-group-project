Privacy & security

Cameras in your home are personal. These are the controls WHSS commits to, written to align with Canada's private-sector privacy law (PIPEDA).

🔐 Encryption everywhere

Video and sensor events are encrypted on the device, in transit and at rest. Customers can turn on end-to-end encryption so that not even WHSS can view footage.

👤 Least-privilege staff access

Employees and contractors cannot browse customer video. Access requires the customer's consent for a specific support ticket, and every view is logged and audited.

📱 Two-step login by default

Every account uses two-step verification, which blocks attackers who reuse passwords stolen from other websites.

🍁 Data kept in Canada

Account data and cloud video are stored in Canadian data centres (a proposed requirement for any software-as-a-service vendor WHSS selects).

🗓️ Clear retention

Cloud video is deleted automatically after 30 or 60 days depending on plan. Customers can download or delete it sooner.

🚫 No secret AI training

Customer video is never used to train algorithms without separate, opt-in consent.

Why these controls matter

In a 2023 complaint, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission charged that Ring, Amazon's home-camera business, failed to restrict employees' and contractors' access to customer videos, used videos to train algorithms without consent, and lacked security protections against hackers. In 2024 the FTC sent more than US$5.6 million in refunds to Ring customers under the settlement.[6] Canada's Privacy Commissioner has published guidance telling makers of connected devices such as cameras, doorbells and alarms how PIPEDA's privacy principles apply to them.[10]

Key takeaways

  • Problem: WHSS's data shows its factory works well (98.5% yield), but its information flow fails: 27.8% of supplier deliveries on time, 13.3% of customer orders on time and in full.
  • Chapter 13 answer: plan the system around strategy, justify it with NPV, ROI, break-even and a business case, then choose how to acquire it.
  • Recommendation: lease a cloud ERP with supply chain and inventory modules (SaaS), configured rather than custom-built, rolled out with a pilot and then in phases.
  • Business case: about a 9-month payback and a 5-year NPV near $195K. It stays positive if WHSS captures at least ~41% of the estimated benefits.
  • Biggest risks: poor change management, scope creep and vendor privacy practices. Canada's Phoenix pay system shows the cost of skipping a pilot.
How to read this page: plain paragraphs report facts from cited sources (numbers in brackets link to the reference list). Blue "Our analysis" boxes are our group's own interpretation and estimates.

1. Research question

Chapter 13 explains how organizations plan for, justify, acquire and implement information systems. It covers IT strategic planning, cost-benefit analysis, the decision to buy, lease or build, and the systems development life cycle (SDLC), along with faster alternatives such as prototyping, Agile and low-code tools.[1]

We apply those ideas to one practical question: WHSS is missing delivery promises to its customers and from its suppliers. What information system should it acquire to fix this, how should it pay for it, and how should it roll it out?

2. The problem in WHSS's data

We analyzed WHSS's procurement, production and fulfillment records from October 2025 to February 2026.[2]

Our analysisWHSS does not have a making problem. It has a planning and information problem. Material shortages caused 25 of the 132 downtime events on the shop floor, stock was below safety level in more than half of the weekly snapshots, and over half of orders left the building late before a carrier ever touched them. These are symptoms of disconnected purchasing, inventory and order data, often kept in spreadsheets. That is the kind of problem an integrated information system solves. 3. IT planning: aligning the system with strategy

Chapter 13's planning process starts with the organization's mission and strategic plan. It then assesses the current IT architecture, sets an IS strategic plan, and turns that into an IS operational plan and specific development projects.[1]

Mission & business assessment→Strategic plan→IS strategic plan→New IT architecture→IS operational plan→IS projects

The chapter says an IT strategic plan must meet three objectives: (1) align with the organization's strategic plan; (2) provide an IT architecture that connects users, applications and databases; and (3) allocate development resources efficiently so projects finish on time and on budget.[1]

ObjectiveApplied to WHSS
Align with strategyWHSS's stated goals are reliable availability, low returns and on-time delivery. The system must directly support those three, not just "modernize IT."
Connected architecturePurchasing, inventory, production and customer orders must share one database so a late supplier shipment updates the promise date a customer sees.
Efficient resource allocationA ~100-person firm has little IT staff, so the plan should favour options that need minimal internal programming.

WHSS's IS operational plan

Chapter 13 lists the parts of a typical IS operational plan: the mission, the IS environment, the IS function's objectives and constraints, the application portfolio, and resource allocation and project management.[1] A first draft for WHSS:

Plan elementWHSS draft (our analysis)
Mission of the IS functionGive every department one accurate, shared view of supply, inventory and orders so WHSS can keep its delivery promises.
IS environmentDisconnected tools today: separate purchasing, production and order records, joined only by IDs such as SupplierID, ItemID and SO_ID.
ObjectivesRaise on-time supplier receipts from 27.8% and customer OTIF from 13.3%; cut weeks below safety stock from 54.5%.
ConstraintsSmall IT staff, limited capital budget, US-dollar exposure, and privacy duties under PIPEDA.
Application portfolioPriority 1: cloud ERP (procurement, inventory, sales orders). Priority 2: e-commerce site and CRM. Priority 3: AI demand forecasting.
Resource allocation & project managementOne project sponsor (operations), one part-time system administrator, an external implementation partner, and a phased budget released at each go/no-go gate.

4. Justifying the investment: cost-benefit analysis

The textbook lists four common ways to justify an IT investment: net present value (NPV), return on investment (ROI), break-even analysis, and the business case approach.[1] Benefits are often harder to measure than costs, so assumptions must be stated openly.

For costs, we priced a cloud ERP subscription. Odoo's published Standard plan, for example, lists about US$31.10 per user per month on annual billing.[9] We converted at C$1.33 per US dollar, the average rate in the WHSS dataset.

Our analysisWith our base estimates, the system pays for itself in under a year and has a five-year NPV near $195K at 8%. The more useful finding is the break-even point: NPV stays positive as long as WHSS captures roughly 40% or more of the estimated benefits. That margin of safety is what the business case approach should stress to management. It also shows why the chapter warns that intangible benefits must be argued carefully.

5. The four fundamental acquisition decisions

Chapter 13 frames every acquisition around four questions.[1] Our answers for WHSS:

DecisionWHSS answer
How much computer code does the company want to write?Very little. WHSS's needs (purchasing, inventory, manufacturing orders, sales orders) are standard. Configuration beats custom code.
How will the company pay for the application?Subscription (lease). This turns a large up-front capital cost into a predictable operating expense, which suits a small firm's cash flow.
Where will the application run?In the vendor's cloud (software-as-a-service), with a requirement for Canadian data hosting.
Where will the application originate?An established vendor, with a local implementation partner. It should not be built in-house.

This matches broader Canadian trends. Statistics Canada reported that cloud computing was the most-used information and communication technology among Canadian businesses in 2023, at 48%.[3] The Canadian Federation of Independent Business (CFIB) found that 92% of small businesses use digital tools but only about 10% have fully integrated them across operations, with cost, time and skills as the main barriers.[4]

6. Acquisition options compared

The chapter describes several ways to acquire an application: buy a prewritten package, customize one, lease it (including from an application service provider or software-as-a-service vendor), use open-source software, outsource, or develop it in-house.[1] The table below weighs each for WHSS.

OptionAdvantages (Ch. 13)Disadvantages (Ch. 13)Fit for WHSS
Buy prewritten (off-the-shelf)Saves time; can try before buying; know what you getMay not fit exactly; hard to modify; vendor controls updates; integration difficultyMedium
Customize prewrittenFlexibility when specific needs existCustomization can be difficult and costly; upgrades get harderMedium (only light configuration)
Lease / software-as-a-service (SaaS)Affordable for small and medium firms; includes common features; vendor runs itMay not fit requirements exactly; depends on vendor and internetHigh: recommended
Application service provider (ASP)Vendor hosts a separate copy per customerLess economical than multi-tenant SaaSLow
Open-source (e.g., Odoo Community)No licence fee; source code can be changedWHSS must host, secure and support it itselfMedium (backup option)
Outsource developmentAccess to outside expertiseLess control; communication and quality riskLow
Custom development in-houseExact fit; potential competitive advantageMost expensive and slowest; needs scarce developers; highest riskLow
Our analysisThe deciding factors are WHSS's size and the nature of its problem. Supplier tracking, reorder points and order promising are common processes that every ERP handles. Custom development is justified only when a process is a source of competitive advantage, and WHSS's advantage is its products and local service, not its purchasing software. SaaS gives WHSS standard best-practice processes at a cost it can afford.

ASP vs. SaaS: what's the difference?

Figures 13.2 and 13.3 in the chapter contrast the two hosted models.[1] An application service provider runs a separate copy of the application and database for each customer. A software-as-a-service vendor runs one copy of the application for all customers, with each customer's data kept apart inside a shared database. Because SaaS spreads one system across many customers, it is usually cheaper and updated more often, which suits a small firm like WHSS.

7. The systems development life cycle (SDLC) for WHSS

Even when a system is leased, the chapter's six SDLC stages still structure the project: investigation, analysis, design, programming and testing, implementation, and operation and maintenance.[1]

StageDeliverableWhat WHSS does
1. Systems investigationGo / no-go decisionCompare the three basic solutions: do nothing, modify the current tools, or acquire a new system. Then run a feasibility study. Technical: can a SaaS ERP connect to the web store and carriers? Economic: the cost-benefit analysis above. Behavioural: will buyers, planners and warehouse staff use it?
2. Systems analysisUser requirementsDocument current processes and pain points: late POs, stockouts, missed promise dates. Gather requirements from each department.
3. Systems designTechnical specificationsConfigure modules, reorder rules, supplier scorecards and approval workflows. Freeze scope to prevent scope creep.
4. Programming & testingTested systemLimited coding (integrations only). Test with real WHSS data: suppliers, items, bills of materials, open orders.
5. ImplementationWorking systemConvert from the old tools (see conversion strategies below) and train users.
6. Operation & maintenanceOngoing supportAll three kinds of maintenance: debugging (fixing errors found after go-live), updating (new tax rates, new suppliers, vendor releases) and adding features, such as AI demand forecasting later.

Who is involved, and when

Figure 13.5 in the chapter shows that user and developer involvement shift over the SDLC: users are most involved at the start (investigation and analysis) and again at the end (implementation and operation), while developers dominate the middle (design, programming and testing).[1] The chapter also names the people involved: systems analysts, programmers, technical specialists and other stakeholders.[1]

Our analysisFor WHSS this means buyers, planners and warehouse leads must be booked into the project at the start (requirements) and the end (testing with real data, training, go-live). In the middle, the vendor and implementation partner do most of the work. The most common mistake is letting users disappear during the middle and then rejecting the system at go-live.

Choosing a conversion strategy

StrategyHow it worksRiskFit for WHSS
DirectOld system off, new system on, same dayHighestAvoid
PilotNew system used in one part of the business firstLowRecommended: start with procurement for one product family
PhasedIntroduced module by moduleMediumRecommended after the pilot: procurement → inventory → sales orders
ParallelOld and new run side by sideLowest, but costlyOnly for financial data during cut-over

A Canadian warning: the federal government's Phoenix pay system shows what happens when implementation shortcuts are taken. Phoenix was meant to replace a 40-year-old federal pay system. In its 2018 audit, the Office of the Auditor General of Canada concluded that the project was an incomprehensible failure of project management and oversight, and found that a planned pilot in one department had been cancelled before the system went live.[7] By October 2022, The Canadian Press reported that Phoenix had cost the government more than $2.1 billion.[13] In March 2026, the Auditor General reported that replacing Phoenix with a new system (Dayforce) was estimated at over $4.2 billion, and called that estimate rough.[8]

Our analysisPhoenix is the direct-conversion risk at national scale. For WHSS the lesson is cheap to apply: pilot first, and set a clear go-live test (for example, two weeks of POs with no errors) before expanding.

8. Alternative development methods & tools

Chapter 13 also covers faster methods: joint application design (JAD), rapid application development (RAD), Agile, DevOps and end-user development. It lists tools such as design thinking, prototyping, CASE tools, component-based development, containers and low-code platforms.[1]

Development methods

MethodWhat it isHow WHSS would use it
Joint application design (JAD)A group workshop where users and analysts agree on requirements together, instead of separate interviews.One or two workshops with purchasing, production, warehouse and sales staff to set reorder rules and approval steps.
Rapid application development (RAD)Builds working versions quickly and refines them with user review in short cycles (Figure 13.6).Configure a working procurement screen in the pilot, let buyers try it, adjust, repeat.
Agile developmentDelivers the system in small, usable pieces over short iterations, with priorities reset after each one.Two-week sprints: supplier scorecard first, then reorder alerts, then order promising.
DevOpsDevelopment and operations teams work together so changes are tested and released continuously.Mostly handled by the SaaS vendor, which releases updates on a regular cycle. WHSS tests its own integrations (web store, carriers) after each release.
End-user developmentBusiness users build their own small applications or reports.Planners build their own inventory reports. The risk is uncontrolled "shadow" apps, so IT should review anything that touches customer data.

Development tools

ToolWhat it isRelevance to WHSS
Design thinkingA human-centred approach: understand the user's problem first, then design and test solutions.Watch how an installer actually reorders stock before designing the Partner Portal.
PrototypingShow users a working model early and improve it based on feedback.How we built this website (section 10).

Low-code is growing quickly. Gartner forecast the worldwide low-code development technologies market at US$26.9 billion for 2023, and predicted that by 2026 developers outside formal IT departments would make up at least 80% of low-code tool users.[11] That supports the chapter's point about end-user development, and its risk.

The GoDaddy case (IT's About Business 13.1)

The chapter's GoDaddy case asks about building AI tools in-house and pausing other projects to do it.[1] GoDaddy paused all other engineering work for a six-week AI "surge" that produced about 30 new customer features for its Airo platform. It used a mix of homegrown and third-party AI technology, including Amazon Web Services' Bedrock platform.[5]

Our analysis: answering the case questions for WHSS In-house vs. third-party: GoDaddy is a large technology company for which AI is a core product, so building in-house made sense. WHSS is a hardware assembler with no large engineering team, so its advantages and risks run the other way: it should buy or lease.
Pausing other work: GoDaddy could pause projects because it had many engineers and a clear strategic priority. WHSS should not freeze operations. Its "surge" should be a time-boxed pilot with a small team.
Short-term sacrifice vs. long-term gain: WHSS accepts short-term costs (implementation fees, training time) for long-term gains (better OTIF, fewer stockouts), as long as the gains are tracked against the business case.

10. This website as a Chapter 13 case

Building this prototype was itself a small systems acquisition, and we followed the chapter's concepts:

  • Build vs. buy: we custom-built the site in HTML, CSS and JavaScript because the course asks for a functional prototype. For a real launch, WHSS would more likely lease a SaaS e-commerce platform, for the same reasons given in section 6.
  • Prototyping / RAD: we built working versions of key screens first (kit builder, order tracking), reviewed them as a group, and refined them in short iterations. This is the iterative cycle the chapter contrasts with the traditional SDLC.
  • SDLC stages: investigation (what does WHSS need?), analysis (customer journey and data available), design (page structure), programming & testing (every link, form and lookup tested on desktop and mobile), implementation (publishing) and maintenance (updates after feedback).
  • Using real data: the order tracker and Partner Portal read the WHSS dataset. This shows how a website becomes a front end to transaction and decision-support systems.

12. Recommendation

WHSS should lease a cloud ERP with supply chain and inventory modules (SaaS), configured rather than customized, and implemented through a pilot-then-phased rollout.

  1. Justify: present the business case with NPV, ROI and break-even, stressing that it stays positive at about 40% of estimated benefits.
  2. Select: shortlist 2–3 SaaS vendors. Require Canadian data hosting, data export rights and CAD pricing.
  3. Pilot: start with procurement for the camera product line, where the China-based suppliers create the most delay.
  4. Phase: add inventory reorder rules, then sales-order promising and carrier tracking.
  5. Measure: track on-time receipts, OTIF and stockouts monthly against today's baseline (27.8%, 13.3% and 54.5%).
